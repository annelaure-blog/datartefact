A shipping manifest is a bureaucratic document — a list of goods, their weight, their origin, their destination. It is also one of the richest sources of historical data we have.

## What they record

At their most basic, manifests record commodities: cotton, tobacco, spices, machinery. But they also record routes, timing, intermediaries, and sometimes the names of people — passengers, crew, enslaved individuals transported as cargo.

## What they reveal

Aggregate enough manifests and patterns emerge: which ports dominated at which moments, how trade networks shifted in response to wars or disease, which goods moved together and which never did.

The data historian's task is to read these lists against each other and against the silences — what was never recorded, what was deliberately obscured, what categories the form simply had no space for.

## Limits of the source

Manifests record what was declared, not what was carried. Smuggling, mislabelling, and deliberate omission are part of the record too, even when they leave no trace.
