Mara Singh is a documentary photographer based in London. Since 2010 she has worked with archives across South Asia, East Africa, and the UK, photographing paper-based administrative systems — registers, ledgers, card indices — in the months before they are digitised or destroyed. Her work has been exhibited at the V&A and the Jeu de Paume.

We spoke over email in February 2026.

---

**Your work is often described as archival photography, but you resist that label. Why?**

Because archival photography implies that what I'm doing is documentation — preserving a record of the thing before it disappears. But I'm not trying to replace the original. I'm trying to make visible what the digitisation process will erase: the physical evidence of use.

When you scan a ledger, you get a very good image of the page. You do not get the weight of it, the smell, the water damage on the binding, the pencil annotations in the margins that were too faint to appear in the scan. You do not get the sense of how many times the page was turned. Digitisation is not preservation. It is a translation, and every translation loses something.

**What does a high volume of use look like in a document?**

The edges of pages wear down first. The most-referenced entries are the ones where you see the lightest, most abraded surface — the ink survives because it's embedded, but the paper fibres around it are gone. You can read a document's usage history in its physical degradation.

Indexes are particularly interesting. In a card index, the most-used cards are the thinnest. They have been handled thousands of times. The least-used cards are often pristine — you can read them clearly, but that clarity is also evidence of neglect.

**Does the digitisation process itself leave traces?**

Always. You can tell when a document has been scanned because of the handling required — the cover shows wear patterns consistent with the book being held open flat, which is not how it would have been held during normal reading. The spine sometimes cracks. There is a category of archival damage that is caused specifically by the act of preserving.

**What do you want people to take from your photographs?**

I want them to feel the loss of something they didn't know was there. Most people assume digitisation is straightforwardly good — the document is saved, it's accessible, anyone can read it. I don't want to argue that digitisation is bad. I want to argue that it is not enough to describe a process as preservation without asking: what, precisely, are we preserving, and at what cost?
