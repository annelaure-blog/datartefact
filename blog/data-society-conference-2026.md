The Data and Society Research Institute held its annual conference in New York from 24–26 March. Three days, sixty-odd speakers, and a single recurring argument that nobody quite named but everyone was circling: the politics of deletion.

## The deletion question

It came up first in a panel on historical datasets — specifically, a discussion of whether it is ethical to continue making accessible a dataset of mortality records that was compiled using methods we now consider coercive. The panel did not reach a conclusion, but the shape of the disagreement was instructive.

On one side: the permanence argument. Historical records are irreplaceable. Even flawed data is evidence. Destroying or restricting access destroys the possibility of future correction.

On the other: the harm argument. The dataset was compiled by extracting information from communities that did not consent. Making it freely accessible continues to extract value from those communities. The fact that the original harm cannot be undone does not mean the ongoing harm must be perpetuated.

Neither position is obviously wrong. What struck me was how rarely this question is asked about contemporary datasets — we discuss the ethics of collection, of use, of bias, but almost never of destruction.

## A session on infrastructure

A talk on the physical infrastructure of data storage was one of the conference highlights. The speaker — a geographer, not a data scientist — had spent two years visiting server farms in rural Iceland and Nevada, mapping their relationships to local energy grids, water systems, and land use.

The talk was a reminder that "the cloud" has a physical footprint that is both enormous and carefully hidden. Data is not weightless. Storage is not neutral. The decision to keep a dataset alive is an ongoing resource commitment that someone is paying for — and that someone's interests shape what gets kept.

## What I'm still thinking about

The best conferences give you one question you didn't have before you arrived. Mine is this: who has the right to decide when a dataset has served its purpose? And what does it mean to retire data gracefully, in a way that acknowledges both its value and its costs?

No one at the conference was asking this. They should be.
