 Five days at the National Library

What it's like to spend five days requesting folios, waiting for trolleys, and reading documents no one has touched in thirty years.

The first thing you notice is the trolleys. Every document you request arrives on a metal trolley — sometimes one, sometimes a stack — pushed by a staff member who has no idea what is on it. That indifference is not negligence. It is professionalism. The document does not matter to them. It matters to you.

The request system

The National Library runs a slip-based request system for its oldest holdings. You fill in a paper form — author, title, shelfmark, date range — and submit it at a desk. Nothing is instant. You are told to return in the afternoon.

This waiting is not wasted time. It is part of the work. You plan your next requests. You read the catalogue more carefully. You think about what you are actually looking for, which is rarely what you thought you were looking for when you arrived.

What the catalogue reveals

The catalogue is its own data artefact. It was compiled over decades, in layers, by different hands using different conventions. A shelfmark assigned in 1887 sits next to one assigned in 1994. The classification logic shifts across time — sometimes alphabetical, sometimes thematic, sometimes following no visible principle at all.

Reading the catalogue is itself an act of interpretation. The gaps are as informative as the entries.

What you find

On day three I requested a set of ledgers from a colonial customs administration. They had not been requested since 1974, according to the access record taped to the inside cover. The ledger itself was intact — columns of goods, weights, duties, names — but the access record was a second layer of data: who had looked, when, for what purpose.

No one had thought to study the access record. It was administrative residue. But it told a story about what questions researchers had been asking, and when they stopped asking them.

The value of going in person

You cannot replicate this digitally. Not because the images would be lower quality — they would not — but because the physical handling changes what you notice. The weight of a ledger tells you something about its intended permanence. The quality of the paper tells you something about the resources available. The smell tells you something about the conditions of storage.

These are not marginal details. They are part of the document’s meaning.
